## One-line contribution
A JAX-native, GPU/TPU-parallel, auto-differentiable rigid-body physics engine that co-locates physics simulation and RL optimization (PPO, SAC, ES, and a differentiability-exploiting Analytic Policy Gradient) on a single accelerator, reaching "millions of simulation steps per second" on a single chip and training locomotion policies "in seconds to minutes" (Abstract, Sec. 1).

## Setting
- hand(s): **Grasp** — "a simple pick-and-place environment, where a 4-fingered claw hand must pick up and move a ball to a target location. We include this environment primarily as a proof-of-concept to demonstrate that the contact physics of our engine are sufficient to support nontrivial manipulation tasks" (Sec. 4.2.2). No vendor, DoF count, or real-hand correspondence is given — this is a from-scratch synthetic "claw" morphology, not a modeled commercial hand. Table 1 gives its dimensions: Obs Dim 139, Act Dim 19, continuous. Appendix B (Fig. 6) shows "the first 300 steps of a performant grasping policy... The hand is able to pick up the ball and carry it to a series of red targets. Once the ball gets close to the red target, the red target is respawned at a different random location" — i.e., a repeated-relocation grasp-and-carry task, not a single pick-and-place trial.
- arm: none (the claw hand's own base is presumably floating/actuated directly; no separate arm segment is described). single/bimanual: single manipulator.
- simulator / physics: Brax itself, using **maximal coordinates** (every free body tracked independently via position, orientation, velocity, angular velocity in a `QP` data structure) rather than MuJoCo/SAPIEN-style generalized/joint coordinates (Sec. 3): "Brax simulates physical interactions in maximal coordinates, where every independent entity in a scene that can freely move is tracked separately."
- observation / action space: per-environment dims in Table 1 (verbatim): Halfcheetah Obs 25 / Act 7; Ant Obs 87 / Act 8; Humanoid Obs 299 / Act 17; **Grasp Obs 139 / Act 19**; Fetch Obs 101 / Act 10 — all continuous.
- objects / data: Grasp = one ball object + a sequence of randomly-relocated red target positions (Appendix B); no dataset, no object-set diversity (a single, hardcoded task geometry, consistent with Brax's "initial release" framing, Sec. 4.2).

## Physics
- **Contact model**: rigid, **naive/quadratic pairwise collision detection** — "we currently only use the quadratically-scaling, naive collision detection for any colliders included in a scene" (Sec. 7.2, Limitations) — i.e., no broad-phase acceleration structure; every collider pair is checked. No convex-decomposition or mesh-vs-primitive distinction is described for collision geometry.
- **Solver**: **not LCP-based.** Contacts are resolved with "velocity-level collision updates with Baumgarte stabilization for all of our collision primitives," explicitly inspired by the Tiny Differentiable Simulator (Sec. 7.2): "We did experiment with fully springy, impulsive collisions, but found the motion quality and stability to suffer... We experimented with time-of-impact based collision detection, but... we found it provided little accuracy advantage for the complexity penalty it added to the codebase." Joints are modeled as **spring constraints**, not Featherstone-style articulated-body methods: "physics engines that rely on spring constraints instead of more sophisticated Featherstone-style methods can be brittle and can require careful tuning of damping forces" (Sec. 7.1) — Brax explicitly chose springs for simplicity and states "we will investigate Featherstone methods in future work." The paper explicitly defers LCP-based contact solvers to future work: "We leave more advanced collision physics, e.g. LCP-based solvers, and more efficient collision pruning to a future release" (Sec. 7.2). No solver iteration counts are given (there is no per-contact iterative solve in this velocity-level/Baumgarte scheme as described).
- **Differentiable contacts**: **Yes — this is the engine's central claim.** "It is differentiable, opening the door to new optimization techniques" (Sec. 2); demonstrated via the **Analytic Policy Gradient (APG)** algorithm, which "compile[s] a function that takes a gradient of the loss through a short trajectory" and performs gradient descent directly through the simulated rollout (Sec. 5.1.4). However, the authors report APG "is less mature than the previous three [PPO/SAC/ES], and does not currently produce locomotive gaits, and instead seems prone to being trapped in local minima" — i.e., differentiability is architecturally present but not yet demonstrated to produce competitive policies in this release. "We leave it to future work to fully leverage the differentiability of our engine" (Sec. 7.4).
- **Default timestep / substeps**: the ProtoBuf system-specification example (Appendix A) shows `substeps:1` and `dt:.01` as illustrative config fields — this is a worked example for the spec format, not a stated universal default for all bundled environments, and per-environment `dt`/`substeps` values for Ant/Humanoid/Grasp/Fetch are not given in the excerpted text.
- **Friction model**: not specified quantitatively anywhere in the paper (no friction coefficient, cone, or pyramid detail given for the collision primitives).
- **Penetration handling / exposure**: not discussed as a named, measured, or exposed metric. The Baumgarte-stabilization choice (Sec. 7.2) is a standard constraint-drift-correction mechanism but the paper does not quantify resulting penetration depth or describe an API to query it; the authors instead note inheriting "the known tuning requirements and intrinsic non-physicality of these methods" as a general caveat, without a penetration number.
- **GPU or CPU**: GPU/TPU throughout — this is the engine's design center: "Brax puts a physics engine and RL optimizer together on the same GPU/TPU chip, improving the speed/cost of RL training by 100-1000x" (Sec. 2). Benchmarked accelerators include a 4x2 TPUv3 topology, a range of other accelerators/TPU topologies for the Ant scaling curve, and Colab's free 2x2 TPUv2 (Sec. 6.1). One head-to-head SAC comparison explicitly ran "environments evaluated on CPU and learning on a 2x2 TPUv2" for a fairer apples-to-apples baseline against MuJoCo-CPU (Fig. 4 caption).
- **Throughput**: no single numeric table — figures only, with prose bounds:
  - "For an individual accelerator, Brax reaches millions of simulation steps per second on environments like OpenAI Gym's MuJoCo Ant" (Sec. 1).
  - "By leveraging JAX's vectorization and device parallelism primitives, we can easily scale Brax up to hundreds of millions of steps per second of performance by distributing environment computation within and across accelerators" (Sec. 6.1, Fig. 2: scaling curves on a 4x2 TPUv3 across all bundled envs, and on the Ant env across "a variety of accelerators and TPU topologies" — no exact numeric axis values survived parsing).
  - Rough order-of-magnitude engine comparison (Sec. 6.2, explicitly caveated as unfair): "Brax's Ant environment compiled to and running on a TPUv3 8x8 accelerator (~hundreds of millions of steps per second)" vs. "the typical use case of a practitioner running the OpenAI gym MuJoCo-ant on a single threaded machine (~thousands of steps per second)."
  - Wall-clock training-speed comparison (Fig. 3, Sec. 6.2): "Brax reaches performant locomotion in ten seconds or so, whereas the standard [uncompiled, non-parallel] PPO implementation takes close to half an hour," both evaluated for 10 million environment steps, 5 replicas (seeds) each.
  - Astronaut diagnostic (energy/momentum non-conservation, Fig. 5, Sec. 6.2, following Erez et al. i.e. physics_engine_comparison_2015's protocol): "damping, collisions, and gravity" disabled, limbs randomly actuated "for 1 second with approximately .5 Nm of torque per actuator per step"; for energy, actuators additionally disabled, "every body part a random 1 m/s kick," energy drift measured "after 1 second of simulation," averaged over 128 random seeds, single precision floats. Qualitative results only: "Brax achieves competitive linear momentum conservation scaling owing to its maximal cartesian coordinate representation of positions and symplectic integration scheme. Energy conservation performance is in line with Havok and MuJoCo's euler integrator. Brax does exceptionally well at angular momentum conservation, comparatively." No numeric drift values survived parsing.
- **Robot hands shipped as assets**: one synthetic, non-commercial "4-fingered claw hand" (the Grasp environment) — see the code-mismatch flag above regarding its current availability in the repo.

## Evaluation
- metrics: environment-steps-per-second (throughput, Fig. 2); training-curve reward vs. wall-clock time (Fig. 3, 4); linear/angular momentum and energy non-conservation (Fig. 5, "astronaut" diagnostic).
- headline numbers: see Throughput above; no explicit numeric success-rate or reward value is given for the Grasp task itself (the environment is illustrated only qualitatively via Fig. 6's trajectory snapshots, not a quantitative reward/success curve in the main paper text captured here).
- baselines beaten: on the Ant environment, Brax's compiled/parallel PPO reaches performant locomotion "in ten seconds or so" vs. "close to half an hour" for a standard (uncompiled) PPO implementation, both over 10M environment steps (Fig. 3); on MuJoCo-equivalent SAC training curves (Humanoid, Ant, HalfCheetah), Brax "achieve[s] similar reward in a similar number of environment steps compared to their MuJoCo counterparts," explicitly not claimed as higher-reward, only comparably-shaped learning curves (Fig. 4; HalfCheetah gap discussed in Appendix E).
- real robot? no.

## Limitations stated by the authors (Sec. 7)
- Spring-joint approach: "can be brittle and can require careful tuning of damping forces," with instabilities that "grow as a function of the difference in mass scale present in a problem," and "more 'jitter' in our simulation traces than in a hypothetical corresponding Featherstone simulation" (Sec. 7.1).
- Collision approach inherits "the known tuning requirements and intrinsic non-physicality" of velocity-level/Baumgarte methods (Sec. 7.2); collision detection is naive/quadratic-scaling, with more advanced (e.g. LCP-based) solvers and pruning "left to a future release" (Sec. 7.2).
- JIT compilation time "can sometimes approach or exceed the training time for complicated environments (i.e., compilation can take minutes)" (Sec. 7.3).
- APG (the differentiable-optimization showcase) "does not currently produce locomotive gaits" and "seems prone to being trapped in local minima" (Sec. 5.1.4, 7.4); "we leave it to future work to fully leverage the differentiability of our engine" (Sec. 7.4).
- Environments are not perfectly identical to their MuJoCo counterparts "owing to subtle engine differences" (Sec. 4.2.1), with specific gaps documented per-environment in Appendix E (Halfcheetah, Ant, Humanoid).
- Social-impact section explicitly raises a dual-use / induced-demand concern: "by releasing a significantly faster engine, we inadvertently dramatically increase the compute spent on reinforcement learning problems, in much the same way building a new highway in a city can counter-intuitively increase traffic" (Sec. 7.5).

## Notes for the survey (which sections this feeds; contradictions with other notes)
- Physics-engine section: Brax is the clearest "differentiable-by-design, spring/Baumgarte contact, no LCP" data point in this batch — a direct methodological contrast to the MuJoCo notes (mujoco_2012, mujoco_convex_contact_2014), which use soft-convex complementarity-free contact with analytic invertibility, not gradient-through-rollout differentiability, and to isaacgym_2021/orbit_2023's PhysX-GPU pipelines, which are rigid-LCP-family and not differentiable. Brax's own authors are candid that its differentiability is not yet exploited to competitive effect (APG limitation, Sec. 5.1.4/7.4) — flag this when comparing "differentiable simulators" claims across the batch: Brax has the *capability*, mujoco_playground_2025's MJX has the *capability* (via JAX) but neither paper demonstrates a policy trained via analytic gradients that beats RL.
- The astronaut energy/momentum diagnostic (Fig. 5) directly reuses the protocol from physics_engine_comparison_2015 ("Following Erez et al.") — good cross-reference point for the survey's engine-comparison section; results are qualitative only in this paper (no numeric drift values survived parsing) so do not present Fig. 5 as giving precise cross-engine numbers.
- CODE-VS-PAPER FLAG (repeat for emphasis): the paper's only dexterous-manipulation asset (Grasp, the 4-fingered claw hand) is absent from the current code parse's file list — treat any claim about "Brax's Grasp environment" as historical (2021-era) unless independently reconfirmed against the live repository; do not assume current Brax ships a dexterous-hand environment based on this paper alone.
- Throughput figures are qualitative/order-of-magnitude ("millions," "hundreds of millions," "~thousands") rather than precise measured tables — weaker evidentiary quality than, e.g., mujoco_playground_2025's or maniskill2_2023's exact FPS tables; use Brax's numbers only as rough scale-of-speedup context in the survey, not as precise comparison points.
