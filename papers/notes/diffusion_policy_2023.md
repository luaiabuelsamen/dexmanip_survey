## One-line contribution
Represents a visuomotor policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process over action sequences (not single actions), combined with receding-horizon action-sequence execution and end-to-end visual conditioning, beating prior BC baselines (LSTM-GMM, IBC, BET) by an average 46.9% success-rate improvement across 15 tasks / 4 benchmarks (Abstract; Sec. 5; App. B.2: "avg improvement = 0.46858 ≈ 46.9%").

## Method
- paradigm: IL (behavior cloning) ; algorithm: DDPM (Ho et al. 2020) reformulated as a conditional action-sequence denoiser, with a CNN or transformer noise-prediction network εθ.
- Loss: the paper states the DDPM objective in prose: "The training process starts by randomly drawing unmodified examples, x0, from the dataset. For each sample, we randomly select a denoising iteration k and then sample a random noise εk with appropriate variance for iteration k. The noise prediction network is asked to predict the noise from the data sample with noise added... minimizing the loss function in Eq 3 also minimizes the variational lower bound of the KL-divergence between the data distribution p(x0) and the distribution of samples drawn from the DDPM q(x0)" (Sec. 2.2). The equation itself (Eq. 3), recovered by OCR from papers/md/diffusion_policy_2023.ocr.md (noisier than the layout parse, symbols may be imperfect), quoted verbatim: "L = MSE(εk, εθ(x0 + εk, k))" — i.e. a plain MSE between the sampled noise and the network's prediction of it from the noised sample, conditioned on the denoising iteration k, confirming the prose description was exact and not, e.g., a weighted or clipped variant. Conditioned on observations: "we use a DDPM to approximate the conditional distribution p(At|Ot) instead of the joint distribution p(At, Ot)" (Sec. 2.3), with the conditional update (Eq. 4) and conditional loss (Eq. 5), also recovered by OCR, quoted verbatim: "Ak−1_t = α(Ak_t − γεθ(Ot, Ak_t, k) + N(0, σ^2I))" (Eq. 4) and "L = MSE(εk, εθ(Ot, A0_t + εk, k))" (Eq. 5, modified from Eq. 3 by adding the Ot conditioning argument). Training-stability argument (Sec. 4.4) frames the same loss as score matching: the noise-prediction network εθ(a,o) "is approximating the negative of the score function ∇a log p(a|o)," independent of the EBM/IBC normalization constant Z(o,θ) that its InfoNCE-style competitor requires. Those three equations, recovered by OCR from papers/md/diffusion_policy_2023.ocr.md (noisier than the layout parse, symbols may be imperfect), quoted verbatim: the EBM policy (Eq. 6) "pθ(a|o) = e^(−Eθ(o,a)) / Z(o,θ)"; the InfoNCE training loss (Eq. 7) "LinfoNCE = −log( e^(−Eθ(o,a)) / (e^(−Eθ(o,a)) + Σ(j=1 to Nneg) e^(−Eθ(o,ãj))) )" with negative samples {ãj}; and the score-matching identity (Eq. 8) "∇a log p(a|o) = −∇aEθ(a,o) − ∇a logZ(o,θ) ≈ −εθ(a,o)" — this last equation is the paper's explicit argument for why Diffusion Policy training is more stable than IBC's EBM: neither its inference (Eq. 4) nor training (Eq. 5) loss requires evaluating the intractable Z(o,θ), whereas IBC's InfoNCE loss (Eq. 7) does via negative sampling. Code: `DiffusionUnetLowdimPolicy.compute_loss(self, batch)` and `DiffusionUnetImagePolicy`/`DiffusionUnetHybridImagePolicy.compute_loss(self, batch)` exist (diffusion_policy/policy/diffusion_unet_lowdim_policy.py etc.) but only the method signature is present in code/md — the body (e.g. an F.mse_loss on predicted vs. true noise) is not recoverable from this corpus; the exact noise scheduler used to define that loss IS given in the shipped config: `noise_scheduler: _target_: diffusers.schedulers.scheduling_ddpm.DDPMScheduler, num_train_timesteps: 100, beta_start: 0.0001, beta_end: 0.02, beta_schedule: squaredcos_cap_v2, variance_type: fixed_small, clip_sample: True, prediction_type: epsilon` (code/md, diffusion_policy/config/train_diffusion_unet_lowdim_workspace.yaml).
- action representation: a chunk/sequence of future actions At (not a single action), in the task's native control space (position or velocity; 6D rotation for position control, axis-angle for velocity control, App. A.2). Actions are normalized per-dimension to [-1,1] (App. A.1).
- prediction horizon and chunk size: three horizon quantities are defined and kept distinct — "To as the observation horizon, Tp as the action prediction horizon and Ta as the action execution horizon" (Sec. 2.3, "Closed-loop action-sequence prediction"); at inference the policy predicts Tp steps but executes only Ta of them before replanning (receding horizon). Typical values (Tab. 7): To=2, Ta=8, Tp=16 for most Robomimic/Push-T/Kitchen tasks; BlockPush uses To=3, Ta=1, Tp=12 (or Tp=5 for the transformer variant, Tab. 8) because its scripted-oracle demonstrations are Markovian and need a different horizon regime (App. A.4). Ablation: "found the action horizon of 8 steps to be optimal for most tasks" (Sec. 5.3); too-long Ta hurts responsiveness, too-short loses temporal consistency (Sec. 4.3, Fig. 5 left).
- observation encoder: "a standard ResNet-18 (without pretraining)" with two modifications — "Replace the global average pooling with a spatial softmax pooling to maintain spatial information" and "Replace BatchNorm with GroupNorm ... for stable training. This is important when the normalization layer is used in conjunction with Exponential Moving Average" (Sec. 3.2). Separate encoder per camera view, timesteps encoded independently then concatenated. Ablation (Tab. 5) found end-to-end fine-tuning beats both from-scratch and frozen-pretrained (ImageNet/CLIP) encoders for a CNN backbone.
- inference-time procedure: iterative denoising ("Stochastic Langevin Dynamics") starting from AtK ~ N(0,I), K iterations to At0, conditioned once on Ot (observation features computed once, not re-encoded per denoising step, "regardless of the denoising iterations" — Sec. 1). Real-time acceleration via DDIM (Song et al. 2021): "decouples the number of denoising iterations in training and inference" — sim benchmarks use the same 100 iterations for train and eval (iDDPM/DDPM, squared-cosine schedule); real-world experiments train with 100 iterations but "use DDIM with 100 training iterations and 10 inference iterations enables 0.1s inference latency on a Nvidia 3080 GPU" (Sec. 3.4) — Tab. 7's real-robot rows record 16 DDIM inference iterations for the reported hyperparameter runs (D-Iters Eval=16), i.e. the two numbers (10 vs 16) come from different passages (Sec. 3.4 prose vs. Tab. 7 hyperparameter table) and are both reported here as stated. At execution: only the first Ta of the Tp predicted actions are sent to the robot before the next replanning cycle (receding-horizon / warm-started with the previous prediction, Sec. 2.3).
- key trick(s): FiLM-based visual/observation conditioning per-layer for the CNN backbone (found better than inpainting-based conditioning on all tasks except Push-T, App. A.4); causal-attention transformer variant (minGPT-derived) for high-frequency/velocity-control tasks; position-control action space synergizes specifically with the diffusion formulation (Sec. 4.2).

## Evaluation
- metrics (exact definitions): success rate for most tasks; Push-T uses "target area coverage" (IoU of the pushed T-block against the goal region), thresholded against "the minimum achieved IoU metric from the human demonstration dataset" for the real-world success criterion (Sec. 6). Reported as "(max performance) / (average of last 10 checkpoints)", each "averaged across 3 training seeds and 50 different environment initial conditions (150 in total)" (Tab. 1 caption) — footnoted exception: "Due to a bug in our evaluation code, only 22 environment initializations are used for robomimic tasks" (Sec. 5.2).
- headline numbers (with the table/figure they come from): average 46.9% success-rate improvement over best baseline (Sec. 5, App. B.2, exact calc: avg = 0.46858). Robomimic state (Tab. 1) e.g. Transport-mh: DiffusionPolicy-C 0.68/0.46 vs. best baseline (LSTM-GMM) 0.62/0.20. Robomimic vision (Tab. 2) e.g. ToolHang-ph: DiffusionPolicy-C 0.95/0.73 vs. LSTM-GMM 0.68/0.49. Multi-stage (Tab. 4): BlockPush p2 DiffusionPolicy-T 0.94 vs. best baseline (BET) 0.71 ("32% improvement" quoted in Sec. 5.3); Kitchen p4 DiffusionPolicy-C 0.99 vs. LSTM-GMM 0.34 ("213% improvement on Kitchen's p4 metric", Sec. 5.3). Real Push-T (Tab. 6): Diffusion Policy end-to-end 95% success / 0.80 IoU vs. human demo 100%/0.84, vs. best LSTM-GMM 20%/0.24 and best IBC 0%/0.25.
- baselines beaten: LSTM-GMM (= BC-RNN, reproduced), IBC (Florence et al. 2021), BET (Shafiullah et al. 2022, numbers from original paper where not reproduced).
- real robot? Yes — UR5 (Push-T, 20 eval rollouts referenced for LSTM-GMM/IBC failure counts) and Franka (Mug Flip, Pour, Spread, bimanual Egg Beater/Mat Unrolling/Shirt Folding, Sec. 6-7); no aggregate real-robot trial-count table beyond the Push-T 20-run comparison is given in the parsed text.

## Limitations stated by the authors
"our implementation inherits limitations from behavior cloning, such as suboptimal performance with inadequate demonstration data. Diffusion policy can be applied to other paradigms, such as reinforcement learning" (Sec. 9) — text truncated at a reference cut-off in the parsed markdown; no further limitations text survived.

## Quotable claims (verbatim, with section)
- "Diffusion Policy learns the gradient of the action-distribution score function and iteratively optimizes with respect to this gradient field during inference via a series of stochastic Langevin dynamics steps." (Abstract)
- "At time step t the policy takes the latest To steps of observation data Ot as input and predicts Tp steps of actions, of which Ta steps of actions are executed on the robot without re-planning." (Sec. 2.3)
- "using DDIM with 100 training iterations and 10 inference iterations enables 0.1s inference latency on a Nvidia 3080 GPU" (Sec. 3.4)
- "Diffusion Policy with a position-control action space consistently outperforms Diffusion Policy with velocity control" (Sec. 4.2)
- "neither the inference (Eq 4) nor training (Eq 5) process of Diffusion Policy involves evaluating Z(o, θ), thus making Diffusion Policy training more stable." (Sec. 4.4)
- "found the action horizon of 8 steps to be optimal for most tasks that we tested" (Sec. 5.3)
