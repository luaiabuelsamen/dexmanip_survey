# ruka_2025 — RUKA: Rethinking the Design of Humanoid Hands with Learning (Zorin, Guzey et al.; RSS 2025)

sources: papers/md/ruka_2025.md [3a19161f] ; code/md/ruka_2025.md [fe4f6080, github.com/ruka-hand/RUKA]

## One-line contribution
A tendon-driven, 15-DoF/11-actuator humanoid hand under $1,300 in raw materials, whose non-linear tendon actuation is made controllable by learned joint-to-actuator and fingertip-to-actuator LSTM+MLP models trained on autonomously collected MANUS-glove mocap data, not by kinematic modelling.

## Hand record
- maker / lab: Anya Zorin, Irmak Guzey, Billy Yan, Aadhithya Iyer, Lisa Kondrich, Nikhil X. Bhattasali, Lerrel Pinto — New York University (title page).
- DoF / actuated DoF (quoted, Table I): "RUKA | $1,300 | 15 | 11 | Tendon | ✓" — 15 DOF, 11 DOA (degrees of actuation), vs. Human 22 DOF, LEAP 16/16, Allegro 16/16, Allegro Xella 16/16, Inmoov 14/5, Shadow 22/20. Breakdown (Sec. III-B): thumb has 3 motors, 1 per joint (CMC opposition, MCP abduction/adduction, IP flexion/extension); each of the other 4 fingers uses 2 actuators that jointly drive PIP+DIP (coupled, "RUKA actuates these joints with a single tendon per finger"); MCP flexion/splay is passive/underactuated ("mimicking the adduction of the MCP joints" via fixed splay + concave palm curvature, not an active DoF — Fig. 3B, Sec. III-B). Underactuated by design; the paper argues underactuation "can provide robust and reliable functionality while significantly reducing weight and mechanical complexity" (Sec. III-B, citing prior work).
- actuation type: tendon-driven, 11 Dynamixel actuators in the forearm ("actuators housed in the forearm and connected to finger linkages using flexible tendons," Abstract). Actuators: "Dynamixel XM430-W210T motors for the thumb and Dynamixel XL330-M288-T motors ... for the other fingers. Higher torque motors are used for the thumb" (Sec. III-C-3). Tendons: "braided fishing line rated for 200 lbs," secured with a slip knot, routed through a PTFE tube in the palm to the motor (Sec. III-C-2); pin joints are "spring-loaded open with an extension spring" (i.e. springs provide extension, tendons provide flexion — Fig. 3C).
- cost of BOM (quote verbatim): "it takes approximately 7 hours to assemble and costs under $1300 USD" (Sec. I); "The total cost of the raw materials needed, excluding tools (a 3D-printer and soldering iron), is under $1,300 USD. There is also a $500 and $900 version of RUKA with varying Dynamixel motors" (Sec. III-A-2). Table I lists RUKA's cost as "$1,300" against LEAP $2,000, Allegro $15,000, Allegro Xella $75,000, Inmoov $100, Shadow $100,000.
- build time: "Assembling RUKA takes approximately 7 hours, and most repairs take under 20 minutes" (Sec. III-A-4). Parts are 3D-printed "in 24 hours using the Bambu Lab X1C" (Sec. III-C-1), separate from the 7-hour assembly.
- weight: not stated (no gram/weight figure given for the assembled hand; the paper only argues qualitatively that tendon actuation "removes actuator weight from the fingers," Sec. IV-D).
- fingertip / grip force: Table III strength tests — RUKA: Pinch 2.74 N, Payload 6.0 kg, DIP/PIP slip 33.02 N, MCP slip 16.15 N; all highest among Allegro Xella (1.62 N/2.2 kg/8.34 N/3.57 N), Allegro (1.60/3.6/17.8/12.2), LEAP (2.45/4.0/25.17/11.63), Inmoov (2.72/3.2/15.08/–). Each hand tested 3 times per test, highest force recorded, averaged across left/right hands (Sec. IV-C-1).
- tactile sensing: none. Stated limitation (Sec. VII, quoted): "The current design of RUKA lacks tactile sensing, which may limit its performance in complex dexterous tasks. Integrating touch sensors could provide crucial feedback for more precise and adaptive manipulation."
- control interface and rate: code (`ruka_hand/control/hand.py`, `controller.py`, `operator.py`) drives the Dynamixel motors over USB-to-serial (Sec. III-C-3, "Communication with the motors is achieved through a USB-to-serial bridge"). Data collection for the learned controllers ran at 15 Hz (Sec. V-A: "we record fingertip positions, keypoints, joint angles, and both commanded and actual motor positions at 15 Hz"). Teleoperation: "we achieve a teleoperation rate of 25Hz" using the learned controller + ArUco arm tracking; "If users prefer not to use the learned controllers and instead directly control the hand via motor positions, the system supports control at 40Hz" (Sec. VI-A).
- licence: a `LICENSE` file exists in the repo file tree (code/md file tree, top level) but its content/type was not captured in code/md — not stated which licence. Paper says only "open-source design and assembly instructions of RUKA, code, and data are available at ruka-hand.github.io" (Abstract) and "RUKA is designed in OnShape, allowing easy sharing and editing of CAD files" (Sec. III-A-4).
- what the paper demonstrates: (1) Kapandji test 10/10 vs. Allegro/LEAP 9/10 each (Sec. IV-A-1); (2) range-of-motion per joint vs. human (Table II); (3) GRASP Taxonomy — "RUKA successfully reproduces 29 out of 33 human hand grasps" (Sec. IV-A-3, Fig. 7); (4) 20-hour continuous durability run without significant precision drop, plus 90-minute motor-temperature stabilization test (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 5); (5) strength tests, Table III (above); (6) controller-architecture ablation (LSTM+MLP vs. MLP vs. k-NN vs. search-based) on Robot- and Human-Validation sets, Table IV; (7) cross-hand controller generalization: auto-calibration (max-motor-range variation 0.5° across 10 runs) and 3 mm average fingertip-position difference transferring a controller to a newly assembled hand; (8) teleoperated in-hand and arm+hand tasks (Fig. 10); (9) policy learning via HuDOR (imitation from in-scene human video + residual policy fine-tuning) on Cube Flipping and Bread Pick-and-Drop, ~40 episodes/task, ~45 min training (Sec. VI-B).

## Setting
- hand(s): RUKA (15 DoF/11 DOA); compared against LEAP, Allegro, Allegro Xella, Inmoov, Shadow (Table I), and a human baseline.
- simulator / physics: code/md "Simulation Files" section (quoted): "We provide `xml` files that can be used in MuJoco. They can be found under `assets` folder." No paper-side simulator use is described (no sim training in this paper; the controllers are learned directly from real hardware/glove data, not simulated).
- observation: MANUS Haptic Glove keypoints, fingertip positions f_t ∈ R^{5×3}, hand-pose keypoints k_t ∈ R^{5×K×3} with K=5 keypoints per finger (Sec. V-A). Joint angles computed as the dot product between vectors connecting consecutive keypoints.
- action space: predicted Dynamixel motor positions per finger (thumb: fingertip-position-conditioned; other 4 fingers: joint-angle-conditioned), Sec. V-B-1.
- objects / data: autonomous random-walk data collection over motor limits, repeated 500 times for the thumb and 300 times per other finger (Sec. V-A); HuDOR task data for Cube Flipping and Bread Pick-and-Drop (Sec. VI-B); data released via OSF (code README "Download data from OSF").

## Method
- paradigm: hardware design + learned inverse-kinematics controller (supervised regression), plus a downstream imitation-learning demo (HuDOR).
- reward or loss: controller training loss is MSE between predicted and ground-truth motor positions, optimized with AdamW (Sec. V-B-1, quoted: "The loss between the predicted and ground truth motor positions is computed using mean squared error (MSE) and optimized with the AdamW [31] optimizer"). HuDOR policy: "Rewards are computed by matching the trajectories of object centroids between the robot episodes and the human demonstrations" (Sec. VI-B) — reward formula itself not given in the parsed text (defined in the cited HuDOR paper, not here).
- key trick(s): fitting a MANUS motion-capture glove directly onto the robot hand (not just the human) to collect large-scale (fingertip/joint position, actuator command) pairs without joint encoders (Sec. I, V-A); LSTM over the past 10 finger states + MLP head per finger (Fig. 8); auto-calibration binary search over motor ranges to handle build-to-build tendon-tensioning variance (Sec. V-B-2).

## Evaluation
- metrics (exact definitions): Kapandji test (classical thumb-opposition test, standard clinical protocol, not redefined in text). Reachability: 250,000 randomly sampled joint configurations per finger/thumb pair, recording touching instances (Sec. IV-A-2). Durability: continuous run hours before precision drop; motor temperature over 90 min. Strength: Pinch (vertical force gauge, thumb+index), Payload (weight added to a curled cloth-bag grip until >15° joint-angle error), Slip (weight added to a hanging-scale grip until >15° joint-angle error), each defined in Sec. IV-C. Controller error: mean positional error (cm) over 3 axes for the thumb fingertip, on held-out Robot Validation and Human Validation sets (Table IV, Sec. V-B-2).
- headline numbers: Kapandji 10/10; GRASP Taxonomy 29/33; 20 h continuous durability; Table III strength (see Hand record); Table IV controller comparison — RUKA (LSTM+MLP) errors [0.20, 0.27, 0.22] cm robot-val vs. [0.83, 0.60, 0.54] cm human-val, vs. k-NN [0.13,0.14,0.15]/[0.95,0.42,0.83], MLP [0.53,0.56,0.43]/[1.80,1.90,2.00], search-based [0.55,0.59,0.48]/[2.03,2.00,2.01]; cross-hand transfer average fingertip difference 3 mm; RUKA vs. Inmoov: "increased payload capacity of 87.5% and a 64.2% improvement in the slip test" (Sec. IV-D).
- baselines beaten: LEAP, Allegro, Allegro Xella, Inmoov (Kapandji, strength, durability); k-NN/MLP/search-based controller ablations (generalization to human validation set only — k-NN wins on robot validation).
- real robot? yes, throughout — no simulation-only results reported for RUKA itself in this paper (only the MuJoCo asset is provided as a released artifact, not used for a reported experiment in the parsed text).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Quoted in full (Sec. VII): "Currently, training the model and capturing hand poses relies on the Manus glove, which provides accurate data but introduces a cost barrier for full replication and scalability. Exploring alternative motion capture techniques could improve accessibility. The current design of RUKA lacks tactile sensing, which may limit its performance in complex dexterous tasks. Integrating touch sensors could provide crucial feedback for more precise and adaptive manipulation. To maintain the current design's simplicity, it also lacks abduction at the MCP joints, which may limit the hand's ability to perform dynamic tasks. In future versions, we aim to incorporate this functionality using a compliant ball joint."
