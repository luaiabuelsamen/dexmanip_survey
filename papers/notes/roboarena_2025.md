# roboarena_2025 — RoboArena: Distributed Real-World Evaluation of Generalist Robot Policies (Atreya, Pertsch, Lee et al., CoRL 2025)

sources: papers/md/roboarena_2025.md [0f793633] ; no code (bib github: null; the paper points to https://robo-arena.github.io and https://roboarena.github.io/leaderboard, not fetched)

## One-line contribution
Replaces standardised tasks/scenes with crowd-sourced, double-blind, pairwise A/B real-robot comparisons across seven institutions, aggregated by a task-aware Bradley-Terry model; 612 pairwise comparisons over 7 DROID generalist policies rank them closer to an exhaustive "oracle" ranking than a conventional 17-task/44-episode evaluation (Abstract; Sec. 1; Sec. 5.1-5.2).

## Setting
- hand(s): none. Gripper: Robotiq 2F-85 parallel-jaw; arm: Franka Panda 7-DoF; single arm. Cameras: ZED-mini stereo wrist camera and one or more external ZED 2 stereo cameras; the DROID platform on a height-adjustable mobile table (Sec. 4, Fig. 3).
- simulator / physics: none; all evaluations are real-world. "Simulated evaluation environments" appear only as a contribution line in App. A with no experiments in the paper.
- observation: policy inputs are not specified beyond images and a natural-language instruction; recordings of "all observations and actions" are uploaded to the central server (Sec. 3.1).
- action space: not stated; policies are hosted on remote inference servers, and the authors say remote-inference latency "in practice ... to be negligible for the static manipulation tasks" (Sec. 4.1).
- objects / data: evaluators choose scene, objects and task freely; 4284 policy rollouts "across dozens of scenes and hundreds of task instructions" (Sec. 5.2); 32 sample environments shown in Fig. 11; verb and object-category histograms in Fig. 10.

## Method
- paradigm: evaluation protocol (no policy learning). Policy pool: 7 publicly available generalist DROID policies: pi0-flow-DROID, pi0-FAST-DROID, PG-flow-DROID, PG-FAST-DROID, PG-FAST+-DROID, PG-FSQ-DROID, PG-Bin-DROID (Sec. 5.1).
- protocol (Algorithm 1, Sec. 3.1), verbatim steps: "1: for i = 1 to K evaluations do 2: E requests policies for evaluation from C 3: C samples two policies pi_A, pi_B ~ Pi 4: E rearranges scene and defines task T_i 5: E executes pi_A and pi_B sequentially 6: E provides pairwise feedback F_i(pi_A, pi_B) to C 7: C aggregates {F_i} into global ranking 8: C extracts qualitative policy characteristics".
- blinding: "To ensure unbiased evaluation, the evaluators do not know which policies they are evaluating. In practice, we simply provide them with the IP addresses of remotely hosted evaluation servers." (Sec. 3.1)
- initial-condition control, verbatim: "Importantly, we require the evaluator E to closely match the initial conditions within this A/B policy comparison (while they can choose to change them between separate pairwise evaluations). This ensures that the comparison of policies pi_A and pi_B is fair." (Sec. 3.1). Rollouts run "back-to-back until the task is completed or a fixed timeout is reached" (timeout value not given).
- feedback recorded, verbatim: "a continuous progress score in [0...100] that is proportional to the maximum progress a policy achieved on the task (e.g., 0 for no progress, 100 for successfully executing the task, intermediate values for partial success); a binary, pairwise preference label that indicates which policy the evaluator preferred (we leave it to the evaluator to decide how to determine their policy preference); and a free-form, natural language explanation for why they preferred one policy over the other." (Sec. 3.1)
- ranking statistic (Sec. 3.2): Bradley-Terry, "p(pi_A > pi_B) = sigma(theta_A - theta_B)", extended with task-difficulty parameters tau (one per latent task bucket), marginal bucket probabilities nu, and policy-task offsets psi "which model policy-dependent task difficulty"; "The number of task buckets T is a hyperparameter"; fit by an approximate-MLE EM with clipped Newton updates (App. B, Algorithm 2). The displayed model equation did not survive conversion (Sec. 3.2, between the parameter list and "Critically, all parameters can be learned solely from preference data"). Ties supported (y in {0 = loss, 1 = tie, 2 = win}, App. B.1); partial-success scores can be added as a Gaussian term with sigma_partial (App. B.3). Hyperparameters (Table 1): EM iters 60, buckets T = 60, step clip 1.0, L2 on theta and psi 1e-2, step decay 0.99, tol 1e-4.
- baselines for the ranking: Elo (online), plain Bradley-Terry MLE, and "PROG" = ranking by mean progress score (App. B.4).
- qualitative-analysis tool (Sec. 3.3): GPT-4.5 categorises task and scene from first frames; o3 (`o3-2025-04-16`, App. E) writes a policy report that must cite evaluation episodes as evidence.
- system (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 4): four components: remotely hosted policy inference servers (submitters host their own policies, so "proprietary models can be evaluated without sharing model weights"), evaluation clients (a script that "can be run on any computer that is connected to the Internet and the DROID robot"), an evaluation database (instructions, scores, preferences, language feedback, rollout videos), and a central server that assigns policies and "automatically cancels evaluations after a timeout".
- open-sourcing (Sec. 4.2): separate leaderboards planned for "all policies" and "open-source policies" (the latter "trained only on publicly available datasets, and provide weight access and details for how to reproduce training"); institutions "sponsor" a base budget of evaluations for users without robots.
- incentives and safety (Sec. 4.2): an "evaluation credit" system (one credit per pairwise evaluation run, redeemable for an equal number of comparisons of one's own policy); new policies pass a format check and a supervised "test environment" run before entering the pool.

## Evaluation
- metrics: Pearson correlation r and Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV) of a ranking against the oracle ranking, "following Li et al. [29]" (SIMPLER); MMRV described only as "a ranking metric that takes the performance difference between policies into account" (Sec. 5.2), no formula in this paper.
- oracle: "exhaustively evaluating all policies on all tested tasks and comparing their average progress scores. This was done by asking evaluators to score the remaining five policies after each A/B evaluation on the same task. In total, we use 4284 evaluations to compute this oracle ranking." (Sec. 5.1)
- conventional baseline ("Regular"): "the DROID evaluation procedure used in Pertsch et al. [5], which consists of 17 tasks and 44 episodes per policy" (Sec. 5.1).
- scale: 7 policies, 7 universities, 612 pairwise comparisons (Sec. 1); "more than 600 pairwise real-robot evaluation episodes" (Abstract); 4284 rollouts total including oracle scoring (Sec. 5.2).
- headline numbers (Fig. 6, values read from flattened bar labels; assignment to bars is inferred from the text, which says TASK is best): Pearson r: Regular 0.69; Elo 0.90; BT 0.97; TASK and PROG 0.98 (two bars both labelled 0.98). MMRV (%): Regular 13; Elo 5.7; BT 2.7; TASK/PROG 1.8 and 1.1 (which of the two is 1.1 is ambiguous in the flattened figure; the text only says TASK "leads to the best ranking").
- sample efficiency (Fig. 7, Sec. 5.3), verbatim: "RoboArena converges to high-quality rankings within just 100 pairwise comparisons, matching the convergence speed of conventional robot evaluations, while providing significantly more accurate rankings." Curves plotted up to 1000 episodes; no numeric values in the text.
- distribution shift (Table 2, App. B.6): Regular r = 0.692, MMRV 0.141; RoboArena with simulated drift r = 0.838, MMRV 0.058 (MMRV given as a fraction here, as % in Fig. 6).
- preference vs progress disagreement: "for 11% of all A/B evaluations, the preference feedback did not agree with the partial success feedback" (App. D); examples in Table 3.
- VLM task-categorisation accuracy: GPT-4o 94.6% on 448 samples (Fig. 8, Sec. 5.4); Fig. 9 win rates for pi0-FAST-DROID by category (e.g. 100 vs 33 on one category, 55 vs 46, 57 vs 42), category-to-number mapping is scrambled in the flattened figure.
- ranking result (Fig. 5 right, Sec. 5.2): pi0-FAST-DROID strongest; "expressive action representations outperform simple binning tokenization ... and discrete action tokenization ('FAST', 'FSQ') outperform diffusion policies ('Flow', 'pi0-DROID') in language-conditioned evaluations". Binning "consistently underperform in nearly all tasks, with frequent inactivity" (App. F).
- failure pattern (Fig. 12, App. F): policies "tend to succeed on tasks involving direct object manipulation, such as placing objects into containers ... but often fail in tasks that require tool use or nuanced semantic understanding, such as wiping, cleaning or following detailed multi-step instructions"; deformable objects (folding, draping) and tool actions (wiping, scooping) "often resulting in partial motion or outright frozen behavior".
- baselines beaten: the ranking method beats Elo and plain BT (Fig. 6); the protocol beats the fixed 17-task evaluation on correlation with the oracle.
- real robot? Yes, entirely: DROID Franka/Robotiq, 612 A/B pairs plus oracle scoring = 4284 rollouts; per-policy trial counts are not tabulated.

- prior crowd-sourced robotics benchmark, per the authors (Sec. 2): Dasari et al. [58] aggregated rankings across institutions but "required retraining of policies for every new institution and task at hand, and thus remained limited to a small number of tested environments and tasks".

## Limitations stated by the authors
(Sec. 7) Cross-embodiment: only DROID tested. Controlled experimentation: "makes it challenging to perform experiments that only vary a single condition at a time (e.g. only camera angle, or only object position). As such, RoboArena is complementary to targeted, smaller-scale evaluations that focus on individual axes of generalization". Adversarial evaluators: robustness to random or misleading feedback "not investigated". Over-optimisation ("Gotthart's law" [sic]): future work should check that rankings stay correlated with perceived real-world performance. Also (Sec. 4.1): remote inference may need local inference for dynamic tasks.

## Quotable claims (verbatim, with section)
- "evaluators can freely choose the tasks and environments they evaluate on, enabling easy scaling of diversity, but they are required to perform double-blind evaluations over pairs of policies." (Abstract)
- "By not relying on identical initial conditions beyond the horizon of a single pairwise policy comparison, RoboArena foregoes many of the practical challenges prior robot evaluation frameworks faced." (Sec. 1)
- "Standard BT models would simply treat task-related effects as noise, leading to worse rankings." (Sec. 3.2)
- "conventional robot evaluations ('Regular'), which due to reproducibility challenges are restricted to a relatively small number of tasks and environments, do not provide a reliable performance estimate for generalist policies." (Sec. 5.2)
- "We find that evaluators regularly score both policies in an A/B comparison with the same progress score, yet express clear preference for one policy over the other, e.g., because it acted more swiftly or confidently ... Thus, progress-based and preference-based rankings are complementary, and should both be reported." (Sec. 5.2)
- "tasks involving direct object manipulation (e.g., pick-and-place, pushing, toppling, and simple open/close tasks) are more reliably solved than those requiring tool use, cloth manipulation or complex semantic understanding" (App. F)

## Notes for the survey (which sections this feeds; contradictions with other notes)
- Evaluation section: the concrete protocol items to cite are (i) pairwise, double-blind, back-to-back A/B with matched initial conditions only within a pair; (ii) three feedback channels: progress score 0-100, binary preference, free-form explanation; (iii) task-aware BT ranking scored by Pearson r and MMRV against an oracle; (iv) about 100 pairwise comparisons for a stable ranking of 7 policies (Fig. 7). No confidence intervals are reported anywhere in the paper; trial counts per policy are not given.
- Contrast with colosseum_2024 and kress_gazit_policy_eval_2024: RoboArena explicitly gives up single-axis perturbation studies (Sec. 7) and positions itself as complementary to them. Contrast with simpler_2024: RoboArena borrows MMRV and r from SIMPLER but applies them real-vs-real (crowd ranking vs oracle) rather than sim-vs-real.
- Relevance to dexterous hands: none of the hardware is a multi-fingered hand; the paper's claim is that the protocol "readily extends to other robot embodiments" (Sec. 4), unverified. The DROID-specific policy pool (7 VLAs) is not comparable to any dexterous-hand result.
- Figure 6 bar labels are flattened in the markdown; quote only Regular 0.69 / 13% and "best method 0.98 / about 1%" unless the PDF is checked.
