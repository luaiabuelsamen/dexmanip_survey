# humanoid_sim2real_recipe_2025 — Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Based Dexterous Manipulation on Humanoids (Lin, Sachdev, Fan, Malik, Zhu, CoRL 2025)

sources: papers/md/humanoid_sim2real_recipe_2025.md [4310f020 / arxiv 2502.20396] ; no code

## One-line contribution
A practical sim-to-real RL recipe for vision-based, contact-rich bimanual dexterous manipulation on a humanoid: automated real-to-sim system identification ("autotune"), a keypoint/"contact sticker"-based reward decomposing tasks into contact goals + object goals, task-aware human-play initialization plus divide-and-conquer single-task-to-generalist policy distillation, and a hybrid sparse+dense object representation for vision-based transfer (Abstract, §3, Fig. 2).

## Setting
- hand(s): Fourier GR1 humanoid, two multi-fingered hands. Main experiments use Fourier hands: "6 actuated DoFs and 5 underactuated DoFs" each; cross-embodiment generalization also shown on Inspire hands: "6 actuated DoFs and 6 underactuated DoFs" each (§4.1). Both multi-fingered, not grippers; "substantially different masses, surface frictions, finger and palm morphologies, and thumb actuations" between the two hand types (§4.1). Arm: 7 DoF per arm. Bimanual, fixed torso (a stationary/tabletop humanoid setup, no locomotion described).
- simulator / physics: NVIDIA Isaac Gym (§4.1). Autotune calibrates simulator physics params (friction, damping) and URDF constants (link inertia, joint limits) jointly, "under four minutes (or 2000 simulated steps in 10 Hz)" (§3.1) — i.e. control/tuning runs at 10 Hz for the autotune procedure specifically; the trained policy's perception pipeline runs at 5 Hz "to match the neural network policy's control frequency" (§4.1). Objects modeled as simple geometric primitives (cylinders, cubes, spheres) with randomized physical parameters rather than exact meshes (§3.1).
- observation: RL specialist policy observation = "object position and robot joint position at each time step" (Appendix §6.3); asymmetric-actor-critic privileged critic additionally sees arm joint velocities, hand joint velocities, all fingertip positions, object orientation, object velocity, object angular velocity, and the object mass/friction/shape randomization scales (§6.3). Generalist (distilled) policy observation is reduced to "robot joint states and selective object states, including 3D object position and egocentric depth view, since privileged information is unavailable for sim-to-real transfer" (§6.4) — a hybrid sparse (3D center-of-mass from a third-person camera, via SAM2 segmentation + depth) + dense (segmented egocentric depth image) object representation (§3.4, §4.1).
- action space: specialist RL policy action = robot joint angles (§6.3). Distilled generalist Diffusion Policy output = "normalized 7 DoF absolute desired joint positions of each humanoid arm and the 6 DoF normalized (0 to 1) desired joint positions of each humanoid hand" (§6.4).
- objects / data: three tasks — Grasp-and-reach (single-hand, diverse tabletop objects varying shape/mass/volume/friction/color/texture), Box lift (bimanual, box too large for one hand, size/color/mass/pose varied), Bimanual handover (one hand grasps, hands off to the other hand which cannot reach the object directly, objects vary in color/size/mass/pose) (§4.2). Task-aware initialization data: human "play" states (object poses + robot joint positions) collected via teleoperation in sim, "less than 30 seconds per task" (§3.3) — not full demonstrations.

## Method
- paradigm: RL (specialist per-task/per-object policies trained with PPO) + divide-and-conquer distillation into a generalist policy via Diffusion Policy trained on filtered successful RL rollouts treated as "demonstrations" (§3.3, §6.4) — a hybrid RL-then-IL-distillation pipeline, not pure end-to-end RL and not pure IL.
- algorithm: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with asymmetric actor-critic for specialist policies (§6.3); Diffusion Policy (citing Chi et al. [42]) for the distilled generalist, using the same square-cosine noise schedule and 100 diffusion steps as the original Diffusion Policy paper, ResNet-18 (BatchNorm replaced with GroupNorm) for depth encoding, 3-layer MLP (512,512,512) with ELU for proprioception+object-position encoding, AdamW (lr 1e-4, weight decay 1e-5, batch size 128), EMA of model weights at eval (§6.4). Both actor and critic in the PPO stage are 3-layer MLPs with units (512,512,512) (§6.3).
- teacher-student / privileged->vision distillation: yes, explicitly — "we reduce the choices of observation inputs to the robot joint states and selective object states... since privileged information is unavailable for sim-to-real transfer" (§6.4); privileged information given only to the PPO critic (not the actor) is: arm/hand joint velocities, all fingertip positions, object orientation/velocity/angular velocity, and the object mass/friction/shape randomization scales (§6.3). For each sub-task specialist, "we evaluate for 5000 steps over 100 environments, saving trajectories filtered by success at episode reset on the hard disk" then distill via Diffusion Policy (§6.4) — filtered-successful-rollout distillation, not DAgger-style online distillation.
- domain randomization (quoted from Table 4, Appendix §6.3): Object Mass [0.03, 0.1] kg; Object Friction [0.5, 1.5]; Object Shape ×U(0.95,1.05); Object Initial Position +U(-0.02,0.02) cm; Object Initial z-orientation +U(-0.75,0.75); Hand Friction [0.5,1.5]; PD Controller P Gain ×U(0.8,1.1); PD Controller D Gain ×U(0.7,1.2); Random Force Scale 2.0, Probability 0.2, Decay Coefficient 0.99 every 0.1s; Object Position Observation Noise 0.02; Joint Observation Noise +N(0,0.4); Action Noise +N(0,0.1); Frame Lag Probability 0.1; Action Lag Probability 0.1; Depth Camera Position Noise 0.005 cm; Depth Camera Rotation Noise 5.0 deg; Depth Camera Field-of-View randomization 5.0 deg.

## Contact / penetration handling
Contact is central to the reward, but only as a *goal-reaching* term (finger-to-marker distance), not a penetration penalty. Quoted reward (§3.2): "contact goal, in terms of reward, can then be specified as r_contact = Σ_i [1/(1+α·d(X^L,F_i^L)) + 1/(1+β·d(X^R,F_i^R))], where X^L∈R^(n×3) and X^R∈R^(m×3) are the positions of contact markers specified for left and right hands, F^L∈R^(4×3) and F^R∈R^(4×3) are the position of left and right fingertips, α and β are scaling hyperparameters, and d is a distance function defined as d(A,x) = min_i ||A_i - x||_2." "Contact stickers" are procedurally-placed surface markers on simulated objects defining desired contact locations, and different marker placements were shown to elicit different qualitative grasp behaviors (Fig. 5, box lift with markers at left/right centers vs. top/bottom centers vs. bottom edges). No interpenetration measurement, hand-hand collision penalty, or hand-object penetration metric of any kind is reported — the paper's contact treatment is entirely about encouraging correct/desired contact, not discouraging or measuring excess penetration.

## Coordination and embodiment (survey fields)
- two-hand coordination: mixed by task, not a fixed architecture — grasp-and-reach is single-hand (scripted vision module picks the closer hand at initialization, §4.2); box lift and bimanual handover require both hands. For handover specifically, coordination is reward-driven via an explicit staged formulation with a discrete stage variable a∈{0,1} switching which hand's fingertip positions (s_hA vs s_hB) are used in the reward, plus completion bonuses that increase in scale as the stage progresses (Appendix §6.2): "the handover task can be defined with the following goal states: (1) one hand's finger contact with the object; (2) object being transferred to an intermediate goal position while still in contact with the first hand; (3) the second hand's finger contact with the object; (4) object being transferred to the final goal position." This is a single policy per task controlling both arms/hands jointly (one policy over both hands), with coordination emerging from the staged reward rather than from an explicit leader/follower role assignment or relative-frame observation design.
- torso: fixed — Fourier GR1 humanoid described only via its two 7-DoF arms and two hands; no locomotion, base motion, or mobile-manipulation component appears anywhere in the paper.
- hand model/DoF each side: two hand types tested, matched on both sides within a given experiment — Fourier hands (6 actuated + 5 underactuated DoF) for the main results, Inspire hands (6 actuated + 6 underactuated DoF) for the cross-embodiment generalization check; both multi-fingered.

## Evaluation
- metrics (exact definitions): per-task binary success (task-specific: e.g. grasp-and-reach success = object placed at goal location; explicit staged "Pickup" vs. "Task Success" sub-metrics for the object-representation ablation, Table 3); "successful policy" for the initialization ablation is defined as one "that achieve[s] over 60% episodic success during evaluation" (Table 2 caption).
- headline numbers: Table 1 (autotune MSE vs. sim-to-real success, 10 trials): Grasp success 8/10 (lowest MSE) -> 3/10 (median) -> 0/10 (highest MSE); Reach success 7/10 -> 3/10 -> 0/10. Table 2 (task-aware human-play initialization, 10 seeds/setting, success = >60% episodic success): Grasping 80% (with) vs 60% (without); Lifting 90% vs 90% (no difference); Handover 30% (with) vs 0% (without) — biggest effect on the hardest task. Table 3 (object representation, sim-to-real transfer): Depth+Position — Pickup 10/10, 10/10, 10/10, 10/10 and Task Success 10/10, 10/10, 9/10, 5/10 across Grasping/Lifting/HandoverA/HandoverB; Depth Only — Pickup 2/10, 0/10, 0/10, 0/10 and Task Success 2/10, 0/10, 0/10, 0/10 — the paper frames this as "improving sim-to-real success rates on novel objects significantly by 80~100%" (Abstract/§1(D)). Divide-and-conquer distillation ablation (multi-object grasp-and-reach, 10 objects, in-distribution eval over 30 trials): mix 90.0%, shape 63.3%, single 40.0%, all 23.3% (§4.5); sample efficiency ranking: single > shape > all > mix. §4.7 System Capabilities (best policy per task, 10 trials per test object, averaged across objects): grasp-and-reach 62.3%, box lift 80%, bimanual handover 52.5%. Abstract also states "achieving 90% success rate on seen objects and 60~80% success rate on novel objects" (a different aggregation than §4.7's per-task numbers — flagged as two different ways the paper reports overall success, not necessarily consistent one-to-one with the 62.3/80/52.5% figures).
- baselines beaten: internal ablations only — no external baseline method is compared; comparisons are autotuned vs. non-autotuned modeling (Table 1), with/without human-play initialization (Table 2), depth+position vs. depth-only representation (Table 3), and single/shape/mix/all task-decomposition granularities for distillation (§4.5).
- real robot? Yes — all headline numbers (Tables 1-3, §4.7) are zero-shot real-world sim-to-real transfer trials on the Fourier GR1 platform; trial counts given above (10 per condition for Tables 1-2, 10 per test object per task for §4.7, 30 for the distillation-granularity comparison).

## Limitations stated by the authors
"The capabilities achieved in this work are still far from the kind of 'general-purpose' manipulation that humans are capable of." Reward design could be strengthened with stronger human priors (e.g. full teleoperated demonstrations) or an alternative controller (e.g. torque control). "Our work uses no novel technique to reduce the sim-to-real gap in dynamics other than applying naive domain randomization. We hypothesize that this could be a reason for the low success rate on bimanual handover task, which is the most dynamic among our collection of tasks." Hardware-constrained: "the dexterity of these hands is far from that of human hands in terms of the active degrees of freedom," attributed to hardware rather than the method, with more capable hands proposed as future work (§6).

## Quotable claims (verbatim, with section)
- "We introduce a simple, automated real-to-sim system identification method to overcome this with less than four minutes of real-world data" (§1(A)).
- "improving sim-to-real success rates on novel objects significantly by 80∼100%" (§1(D)).
- "This effectively brings pure RL closer to learning from demonstrations, where the sub-task policies act as 'teleoperators' in the simulation environment, and the centralized generalist policy learns from curated data" (§3.3).
- "we hypothesize that this could be a reason for the low success rate on bimanual handover task, which is the most dynamic among our collection of tasks" (§6).
