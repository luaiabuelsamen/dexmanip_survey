## One-line contribution
A SAPIEN-based manipulation benchmark of 20 task families (rigid and MPM soft-body, stationary/mobile-base, single/dual-arm) with fully-physical grasping, 4M+ demonstration frames, a GPU MPM soft-body simulator with 2-way rigid-soft coupling, and a render-server architecture that beats Habitat 2.0/RoboSuite/Isaac Gym on visual-RL sample-collection throughput for its PickCube benchmark (Abstract, Secs. 1, 3-4).

## Setting
- hand(s): **none.** "In ManiSkill2, the default robot being controlled is the Franka Emika, also known as Panda. The degree of freedom (DoF) of a single Panda arm is 7" (Appendix B). All manipulation tasks use the Panda's stock parallel-jaw gripper (Tool Center Point "defined at the center point between the gripper's two fingers," Appendix B) or, in the sim-to-real experiment, a Robotiq 2F-140 2-finger gripper on a ROKAE xMate3Pro arm (Sec. 5.3). Shadow Hand, Allegro Hand, and TriFinger appear **only** as the identity of Isaac Gym's manipulation tasks in a comparison footnote: "For IsaacGym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021), we refer to its 4 manipulation tasks in the original paper: Franka Cube Stacking, Shadow Hand, Allegro Hand, and TriFinger" (Appendix G, footnote 5) — these hands are never used inside ManiSkill2 itself.
- arm: Franka Emika Panda (7-DoF), single-arm for most tasks; dual-arm and mobile-base variants exist among the 20 task families (Sec. 1, "stationary/mobile-base, single/dual-arm") but are not detailed with a specific dual-arm robot name in the excerpted sections. Real-robot arm: 7-DoF ROKAE xMate3Pro (Sec. 5.3).
- simulator / physics: **SAPIEN** (Xiang et al., 2020) for rigid-body simulation (i.e., PhysX-CPU under the hood, per the sapien_2020 note — not re-derived here), plus a **custom GPU MPM (Material Point Method) soft-body simulator built in NVIDIA Warp**, 2-way coupled to SAPIEN's rigid bodies (Sec. 3). Table 10 (Appendix G) lists ManiSkill2's own summary row: Rigid-body simulation = "SAPIEN"; Soft-body simulation = "Warp-MPM."
- observation: point cloud, RGBD, and privileged state (Sec. 1); robot proprioceptive info = joint positions, joint velocities, robot base pose, plus gripper TCP pose for two-finger-gripper robots (Appendix C.1).
- action space: a "comprehensive controller suite" (Appendix B) including fixed joints (0 DoF), joint-space and task-space (Cartesian) position/velocity controllers, and a 1-DoF gripper joint-position controller that "force[s] the two gripper fingers to have the same target position" (Appendix B.4.2); a novel demonstration-action-space-conversion tool lets policies trained with one controller reuse demonstrations generated under a different one.
- objects / data: "20 verified and challenging manipulation task families ... with over 2000 objects and 4M demonstration frames" (Sec. 1); Table 10 states ">2144 object models" (excluding assets requiring offline generation) and ">30k demo trajectories."

## Physics
- **Contact model**: rigid-body contact inherited from SAPIEN/PhysX (not re-derived in this paper — see sapien_2020 note). Soft-body contact is a **penalty-force SDF coupling**, described in detail (Sec. 3, "Rigid-soft Coupling"): "Primitive shapes (box, capsule, etc.) are represented by analytical signed distance functions (SDFs); meshes are converted to SDF volumes, stored as 3D CUDA textures." Two coupling variants are supported: evaluating the SDF at MPM **particle** positions and applying penalty forces to particles (ManiSkill2's choice), versus evaluating on MPM **grid nodes** and applying forces to grids (PlasticineLab's approach) — "we find applying particle forces produces fewer artifacts such as penetration" (Sec. 3) — this is the paper's one explicit, comparative penetration-quality claim.
- **Solver**: rigid-body solver is SAPIEN/PhysX's own (not detailed here). Soft-body solver is **MLS-MPM** (Moving Least Squares MPM, Hu et al. 2018), "similar to PlasticineLab ... but with a different contact modeling approach" (Sec. 3). No LCP/PGS/TGS iteration counts are given for either solver.
- **Differentiable contacts**: not claimed for either the rigid (SAPIEN/PhysX) or soft-body (Warp-MPM) path in this paper.
- **Default timestep / substeps**: "500Hz simulation frequency and 20Hz control frequency" is stated as the matched setting used for the cross-framework PickCube throughput comparison (Sec. 4, footnote 2: "we try our best to match the simulation parameters among the frameworks") — this is presented as a comparison-fairness setting, not necessarily every task's default, but it is the only numeric timestep figure in the paper. The soft-body coupling loop runs multiple soft-body substeps per rigid substep and multiple rigid substeps per environment step (Algorithm 1, Appendix D.1: "for rigid step per environment step... for soft step per rigid step...") but no substep counts are given numerically.
- **Friction model**: not specified quantitatively for rigid bodies. Domain randomization is applied to "joint damping and friction of PushChair, OpenCabinetDoor, and OpenCabinetDrawer" (Appendix G, footnote 9) but no coefficient ranges are given. Soft-body material parameters (Table 4, Appendix D.3, exact, all SI units): Grid Length 0.005-0.015, Particle Volume Density 6.2e-8/1.2e-7, Young's Modulus 300-3000 (and separately 1e4/3e5 — table formatting ambiguous, flagged below), Poisson Ratio 0.3, Yield Stress 2e3/1e4.
  - AMBIGUITY FLAG: Table 4's flattened markdown row has 6 numeric slots for what appears to be 5-6 named parameters (Grid Length, Particle Volume, Density, Young's Modulus, Poisson Ratio, Yield Stress); the value pairing above is a best-effort reconstruction from column order and is NOT guaranteed correct — verify against the original PDF table before quoting individual soft-body material values in the survey.
- **Penetration handling / exposure**: penetration is discussed only qualitatively, as a visual/contact-quality artifact of the soft-body coupling choice, not as a measured or exposed numeric quantity: "we find applying particle forces produces fewer artifacts such as penetration" (Sec. 3) when comparing particle-level vs. grid-level SDF force application. No penetration-depth metric or API is described for the rigid-body path.
- **GPU or CPU**: **hybrid.** Rigid-body simulation (SAPIEN/PhysX) runs on CPU, multi-process-parallelized across worker processes (Sec. 4: "The Python language determines that the only way to effectively parallelize execution is through multi-process"); the MPM soft-body solver runs on GPU via Warp, "translate[d from] Python code to native C++ and CUDA" for "performance comparable to C and CUDA" (Sec. 3); rendering uses a GPU render server shared across CPU worker processes (Sec. 4, Fig. 2b) to avoid per-process GPU memory duplication and GPU-CPU-GPU copy overhead. The paper is explicit that this CPU-rigid/GPU-soft split, not a fully GPU-parallel rigid-body pipeline (like Isaac Gym's), is the architecture: "This performance gain is mainly due to the sequential CPU-rigid-body and GPU-soft-body simulation. It is also partially due to a single CPU processor not able to submit CUDA kernels fast enough to keep up with the GPU" (Appendix D.3).
- **Throughput** (exact tables/figures, hardware = 16 CPU cores of an Intel i9-9960X, 128 GB RAM, 1x RTX Titan GPU (24 GB) unless noted):
  - Rigid-body PickCube, 128x128 images, 500 Hz sim / 20 Hz control, best-of-{16,32,64,128,256,512} parallel envs (Table 1a): Total FPS (random actions) — **ManiSkill2-Server 2487+/-24**, ManiSkill2-Sync 942+/-19, Habitat 2.0 1275+/-10, RoboSuite 1.3 924+/-3, Isaac Gym 865+/-35; Total FPS (Nature-CNN policy) — ManiSkill2-Server 2532+/-63, ManiSkill2-Sync 931+/-4, Habitat 2.0 1224+/-13, RoboSuite 924+/-3(sic, same as random-action row per the flattened table — verify), Isaac Gym 835+/-5. Optimal #Envs: ManiSkill2-Server 64, ManiSkill2-Sync 32, Habitat 2.0 64, RoboSuite 32, Isaac Gym 512.
  - GPU memory, PickClutterYCB with 74 YCB objects/scene (Table 1b): at 4 envs, ManiSkill2-Server 4.9 GB vs. Habitat 2.0 6.4 GB; at 8 envs, 5.1 GB vs. 12.9 GB; at 64 envs, 5.8 GB vs. Habitat 2.0 out-of-memory — "the memory usage of Habitat grows linearly as the number of environments increases, while ManiSkill2 requires very little memory to create additional environments since all meshes and textures are shared across environments" (Sec. 4).
  - Headline: "With render server enabled, ManiSkill2 further achieves 2000+ FPS with 16 parallel environments on a single GPU" (Sec. 4).
  - Soft-body (Appendix D.3, exact): "For all soft body environments with rendering, a single environment runs at around 17-18 FPS; 16 parallel environments on a single GPU gives overall 8084 FPS on a single RTX Titan GPU (4x real time)."
- **Robot hands shipped as assets**: none. Only Franka Panda (with parallel-jaw gripper) and, for sim-to-real, ROKAE xMate3Pro + Robotiq 2F-140. Shadow/Allegro/TriFinger hands are Isaac Gym's assets, cited only in a comparison footnote (Appendix G).

## Evaluation
- metrics: task-specific success rates over held-out (unseen) object instances/configurations (Sec. 5, Appendix F); pose-accuracy metrics for AssemblingKits (position and rotation error thresholds, Table in Appendix F.2); sample-collection throughput (FPS) for the simulator-comparison experiment (Table 1, Fig. 3).
- headline numbers: **AssemblingKits (Transporter Network)**: "our version requires pieces to actually fit into holes, and thus our success metric is much stricter... The success rate over 100 trials is 18% following our success metric, and 99% following the pose accuracy metric of (Zeng et al., 2020)" (Sec. 5.2) — a striking illustration that abstract pose-accuracy metrics vastly overstate physically verified success; the exact same 18%/99% split appears again in the detailed Appendix F.2 comparison table (two-finger gripper: 0.18 vs. 0.98/0.99 pose-accuracy variants; suction gripper: 0.15/0.14 vs. 0.93/0.96). **Sim-to-real PickCube**: simulation success rate 91.0% (point-cloud policy, 10M timesteps); deployed to real robot "50 times" — the exact real-world success count is cut off in the excerpted text but the setup (RealSense D415, ROKAE xMate3Pro 7-DoF arm, Robotiq 2F-140 gripper) and sim number (91.0%) are given in Sec. 5.3. **Demonstration-action-space conversion** success rates (Appendix B.5, exact): PickSingleYCB 99%, AssemblingKits 98%, Write 100%, TurnFaucet 80% (with TurnFaucet's lower rate attributed to inconsistent push-based, non-grasping contact with the faucet handle).
- baselines beaten: on PickCube visual-RL sample-collection throughput, ManiSkill2 (render server) beats Habitat 2.0, RoboSuite 1.3, and Isaac Gym (Table 1a) — noting the important caveat in footnote 2: "A fair comparison of different frameworks is still challenging as their simulation and rendering differ in fidelity. We try our best to match the simulation parameters among the frameworks."
- real robot? Yes — PickCube, ROKAE xMate3Pro 7-DoF arm + Robotiq 2F-140 gripper + Intel RealSense D415, point-cloud policy trained 10M timesteps in sim (91.0% sim success), "evaluate this policy in the real world 50 times" (Sec. 5.3) — exact real success count not captured in the excerpted text (truncated at "50 times"; verify against the original PDF/figure before quoting a real-world success rate).

## Limitations stated by the authors (Appendix G, "We are actively working on current limitations of ManiSkill2")
- Photorealism: "the simulation backend (SAPIEN) of ManiSkill2 supports a ray-tracing renderer (Kuafu). However, photorealism is usually achieved at the cost of speed. For example, BEHAVIOR-1K can only achieve 60 FPS with Nvidia Omniverse and require high-end GPUs" — ManiSkill2 instead "focus[es] on physically realistic short-horizon and low-level visuomotor manipulation" and treats ray tracing as an offline/fine-tuning tool, not the default training path.
- Domain randomization is only partial ("included ... for some of tasks"; footnote 9 lists specific tasks with physical-parameter randomization and specific tasks with visual randomization, not a blanket policy).
- Mobile manipulation / scene-level variation is called out as future work: "we are introducing mobile manipulation tasks similar to those in ManipularTHOR ArmPointNav and Habitat 2.0 but with physically implemented base movement and grasp. Those tasks will involve scene-level variations and demand advanced navigation abilities."
- Soft-body simulator: "Deformable objects like cloth are not supported yet" (Appendix G, footnote 10); the soft-body pipeline itself is bottlenecked by "a single CPU processor not able to submit CUDA kernels fast enough to keep up with the GPU," suggesting headroom via "vectorized simulation, which we leave as future work" (Appendix D.3).
- Low task success rates are framed as a feature, not a bug: "The low success rates on many tasks demonstrate that our benchmark poses interesting and challenging problems for the community" (Appendix G) — e.g., the 18% AssemblingKits success rate under the strict fit-in-hole metric.

## Notes for the survey (which sections this feeds; contradictions with other notes)
- Simulator lineage section: ManiSkill2 sits between sapien_2020 (CPU PhysX, no GPU rigid-body parallelism) and maniskill3_2024 (full GPU-parallel PhysX via SAPIEN) — its architecture is a hybrid: CPU multi-process rigid-body + GPU MPM soft-body + GPU render server. Do not conflate its "2000+ FPS, 16 envs" figure with maniskill3_2024's "30,000+ FPS" GPU-native figure — they are different simulation paradigms (CPU-parallel vs. GPU-batched).
- **No dexterous hand at all** — this is the single most important fact for the survey's dexterous-manipulation-simulator inventory: ManiSkill2's only manipulator is the Franka Panda parallel gripper (and Robotiq 2F-140 for sim2real); Shadow/Allegro/TriFinger belong to Isaac Gym and are cited only in a comparison table. Contrast directly with maniskill3_2024, which the survey should check separately for whether dexterous hands were added in the successor.
- The 18% vs. 99% AssemblingKits result (Sec. 5.2, strict fit-in-hole success vs. loose pose-accuracy success) is a strong, quotable illustration of this survey's Rule 2 ("a large effect is a measurement bug until it survives a second, independent measurement" / "the measure a policy optimises is never the measure that judges it") — worth citing directly when arguing for physically-grounded success criteria over pose-threshold proxies.
- Penetration-as-artifact claim (Sec. 3, particle- vs. grid-based SDF coupling) is the only concrete, comparative penetration statement in this note — useful for the survey's soft-body/rigid-soft-coupling contact-fidelity discussion, but it is qualitative ("fewer artifacts"), not a measured penetration-depth number.
