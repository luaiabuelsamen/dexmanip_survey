\subsection{Reporting practice}
\label{subsec:reporting}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering

Most of this literature does test on hardware, which is the check that matters and the one a
simulator cannot substitute for: 89 of the 113 papers report a real-robot experiment. What they
report about those experiments is much less even. \figurename~\ref{fig:reporting} draws six
reporting axes, each against the denominator that belongs to it, and two of the six are the ones a
reader most needs. Without a count of unseen test objects there is no denominator for a claim
about generalisation; without some statement about contact there is no way to tell whether the
hand passed through the object. Those two are the axes the field states least often. Every share
in the figure is a lower bound rather than a rate, for reasons Appendix~\ref{app:method} gives.

The plainest gap is the trial count. Nineteen of the papers that ran a real robot never say how
many times they ran it, and a percentage with no denominator cannot be given an interval, so it
cannot be compared with anything.

Where a count is stated it is small, and it is not always the same quantity. Some papers report a
per-cell count, a fixed number of trials on each task or each condition; others report a grand
total over every cell; a few cannot be assigned to either basis. The two distributions are kept
apart here and in \figurename~\ref{fig:trials}, because pooling them produces a figure that
describes nothing: Hora \cite{hora_2022}'s 240 trials and Visual Dexterity
\cite{visual_dexterity_2022}'s 20 are experiments of comparable size recorded on different bases.

The per-cell count is the one a confidence interval attaches to, and at the sizes this field
uses the interval is wider than the differences being claimed. Writing $\hat{p} = s/n$ for $s$
successes in $n$ trials and $z = z_{1-\alpha/2}$ for the normal quantile at the chosen level, the
Wilson bounds are \begin{equation} \frac{2n\hat{p} + z^{2} \;\pm\;
z\sqrt{4n\hat{p}\,(1-\hat{p}) + z^{2}}}{2\,(n + z^{2})}, \label{eq:wilson} \end{equation} which,
unlike the normal approximation, stays inside $[0,1]$ and does not collapse to zero width at
$\hat{p} = 0$ or $\hat{p} = 1$. At twenty trials per cell, two methods twenty points apart in
success rate are not statistically distinguishable: the two-proportion test on that comparison
returns nothing, and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives the intervals and the test. The modal cell
in this corpus is smaller still. This is the single most consequential fact about evaluation in
dexterous manipulation, and it is why Section~\ref{subsec:protocol} proposes a protocol rather
than a league table.

Reporting has improved a little and the experiments have not. About two thirds of papers from
2025 onward state a trial count, against three fifths before, but the counts themselves have
not moved: the median has sat near twenty trials per cell every year since 2024, and earlier
years rest on too few observations to read as a trend. A field can get better at disclosing a
number without the number itself getting any better.

The denominators also sit on different hardware. The papers that name their own hand describe it
in 79 different ways, unnormalised, so that figure counts strings and not designs;
\figurename~\ref{fig:hands} gives the distribution. A success rate on an Allegro and a success
rate on an Inspire hand are not measurements of the same thing, because the two differ in actuated
joints by nearly a factor of three.

Nor are the criteria the same. DexVerse \cite{dexverse_2026} counts a pick a success when the
cube is ``lifted at least 0.20 m above its resetting height''. Bench2Dex \cite{bench2dex_2026}
requires its terminal predicate to hold for a continuous dwell time, to reject transient contacts.
The Colosseum \cite{colosseum_2024} counts an episode successful ``if the model completes the task
fully''. DexTrack \cite{dextrack_2025} reports every success rate as a pair under two threshold
sets, which can move the same rollouts by nearly twenty points. Among the papers that state no
criterion at all, most have no success predicate to state: they report radians rotated or
time-to-fall and never define a success, which is a fact about the paper rather than a gap in this
survey. Where a criterion is stated it is usually a rubric, a staged partial credit or a deferral
to a benchmark's own definition rather than a threshold, and a rubric is reproducible inside a lab
and not across two.

\label{subsec:axes}
Seven quantities dissociate in the published data, which is why they have to be reported
separately rather than collapsed into one number.

Task success is the first, and binary success discards the difference between a near-miss and
inaction. Snyder et al. \cite{beyond_binary_success_2026} put it plainly: ``a policy that completes
90\% of the task is clearly better than a policy that is frozen the whole time, yet their success
rates would be identically 0\%''. In \cite{kress_gazit_policy_eval_2024} one policy scores in the
teens overall on a pancake task while picking up the spatula and flipping the pancake in every
attempt.

Robustness to perturbation has been measured, though not yet on a hand. The Colosseum
\cite{colosseum_2024} reports a success drop of a third to a half under single perturbation
factors and more than three quarters under all of them together. In \cite{bench2dex_2026} the
leader of the matched condition is not the leader under combined shift. The ranking at the anchor
is not the ranking under shift.

Generalisation to unseen objects is stated by 40 papers, and half of those test on ten objects or
fewer.

Physical plausibility of the contact is the least reported of the six, at 12 percent of the papers
whose notes settle the question. Section~\ref{subsec:plausibility} takes it up.

Sample and wall-clock cost is reported by about a third of the corpus as an environment count and
by fewer as an episode count. RoboPianist \cite{robopianist_2023} is the exception that shows what
disclosure looks like, giving samples per song and hours per run on named hardware.

Real-robot transfer is the one that undoes the rest, because simulated rank order is not real rank
order. AutoEval \cite{autoeval_2025} scores one policy at 6 of 50 in simulation and 47 of 50 on
the real arm for the same task. Badithela et al. \cite{suresim_2025} state the limit directly:
``the simulation-to-real gap precludes rigorous statistical inferences about real-world outcomes
from simulation results alone''.

Reproducibility is the last, and releasing code is not the same as releasing something that
settles a question. Eight papers state a training objective in their own repository that differs
from the one in the paper, which Section~\ref{sec:train:code} and Table~\ref{tab:codegap} set out
file by file. That is the figure this section uses.

\subsection{Physical plausibility}
\label{subsec:plausibility}

One quantity is specific to hands and is almost never reported: how far the hand passes into the
object it is holding. Outside closed-loop control it has been a standard comparative column for
years. Grasp synthesisers fail a grasp that penetrates past a stated depth
\cite{bimangrasp_2024}, retargeting methods report a maximum depth against a baseline
\cite{toporetarget_2026}, and hand--object datasets score a split on depth, intersection volume
and simulation displacement \cite{oakink_2022}. Among learned closed-loop policies almost nobody
reports it, and nobody reports it measured in the physics the policy ran in.

D-Grasp \cite{dgrasp_2022} is the instructive case, because it does report a volume for a pose
its own policy reached, and the way it does so shows where the quantity goes. Inside the
simulator the paper reports zero penetration, since the meshes there are decimated for speed;
the figure it prints instead comes from re-measuring a single frame against the original hand
mesh and the full-resolution object mesh, with, in the paper's words, ``no physical simulation
involved''. The number a reader wants, how deep the hand sits in the object in the physics that
trained the policy, is zero by construction, and the number reported is a geometric
re-measurement on geometry the simulator never used.

The same paper supplies the reason to want the measurement. Its appendix observes that a deeply
penetrating reference grasp can raise a success rate rather than lower it, because ``the objects
can become entangled within the hand mesh and will therefore not be able to fall down'', and
concludes that ``the success rate metric should always be interpreted in combination with the
other metrics''. A success rate with no plausibility number beside it can be read two ways.

There is a reason the gap persists, and it is not difficulty. A quantity a policy optimises
cannot also judge it, because the policy learns the measure rather than the property the measure
stands for. PhysHOI \cite{physhoi_2023} shows the failure in the clean direction: its imitation
reward is maximised by not touching the object at all, because contact perturbs the reference
trajectory. DexTrack \cite{dextrack_2025} shows it from the other side, defining a maximum
penetration depth, applying it only to its input references, and then reporting tolerance of
``severe hand-object penetrations'' as a result. The remedy is to measure with something the
policy never optimised.

Definitions are not shared either, which is the other half of why published figures do not
compare. GRAB \cite{grab_2020} reports an average penetration in millimetres without saying
whether it is a maximum, a mean or a median; OakInk \cite{oakink_2022} supplies the fullest
published vocabulary, separating penetration depth, solid intersection volume and simulation
displacement. A number without its definition is not a number a second paper can be held to.

What would close the gap is one line in a results table: the maximum and mean penetration depth
over the evaluation rollouts, on a dense surface sample, computed by code that never entered the
reward or the termination rule. That is the plausibility row of the protocol in
Table~\ref{tab:protocol}. What this survey supplies there is a measurement and a count; it does not
supply a threshold, and Section~\ref{subsec:what_would_be_true} says where the one in the table is
borrowed from and why it does not bind.

\subsection{Statistical practice, and a proposed protocol}
\label{subsec:statistics}

Kress-Gazit et al. \cite{kress_gazit_policy_eval_2024} give the field's reference protocol, and
it prescribes process rather than numbers: write the success criteria before the run, have someone
other than their author score the runs, match initial conditions with image overlays, interleave
the policies blind within one session, report counts rather than percentages, and use a posterior
over the success probability instead of a point estimate. All of that is right and none of it
fixes the sample size. It prescribes no minimum trial count and no interval width, and its own
worked example shows why one is needed: at twenty evaluations per policy, an eighteen-point
difference in success rate still leaves better than a one-in-ten chance that the worse policy is
the better one. Multiply every count in that example by ten and the same two rates separate.

The TRI LBM Team \cite{lbm_careful_examination_2025} supplies the missing numbers by fiat rather
than derivation: 50 rollouts per task per policy per condition on hardware, 200 in simulation,
blind, with randomised policy order inside per-initial-condition bundles. It replaces confidence
intervals with Beta-posterior violins and corrects its pairwise tests under Bonferroni, ``unless
otherwise noted''. Its own warning is the strongest sentence in this literature: ``there is
significant risk that many robotics papers are measuring statistical noise due to insufficient
statistical power''.

The rest fall short of their own advice. RoboArena \cite{roboarena_2025} runs hundreds of
double-blind pairwise comparisons across seven institutions and thousands of rollouts, and reports
neither confidence intervals nor per-policy trial counts. The Colosseum \cite{colosseum_2024}
evaluates hundreds of test sets with ``one training seed and one evaluation seed'' per baseline and
gives no intervals in simulation. AutoEval \cite{autoeval_2025} calls $\pm 10$ percent ``the
natural variance of robot evaluations'' without giving the formula behind the intervals it
plots.

Two papers do give usable numbers. Badithela et al. \cite{suresim_2025} pair real and simulated
trials and de-bias the simulation with a rectifier, which saves up to a quarter of the hardware
trials when the paired correlation is high and nothing at all when it is near zero; their decision
rule is exact, that combining helps only when the rectifier variance is below the variance of the
real evaluations. Snyder et al. \cite{beyond_binary_success_2026} give the larger saving by scoring
progress rather than success: a sequential test on graded scores cuts hardware trials by about 45
percent, and a gap that needs 80 binary trials to establish is established in 18 when the score is
continuous. That is the strongest argument in this literature for grading partial progress.

\label{subsec:protocol}
Every count below is printed by \path{tools/make_eval_tables.py} in its derivation mode, and each
axis is derived for the statistic that axis actually reports: a single rate takes a Wilson
half-width, a matched comparison takes McNemar, a ratio takes the standard error of the log ratio,
a correlation takes the Fisher-z interval.

Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} derives the counts in Table~\ref{tab:protocol}, and they come to
this. Reporting a single rate honestly takes about 100 real rollouts per cell, which is where the
worst-case half-width of \eqref{eq:wilson} falls to ten points. Comparing two policies is a harder
question: the design is paired, so the count follows McNemar and depends on how often the two
policies disagree rather than on the two rates alone, and separating a twenty-point difference at
conventional power takes 57 matched pairs per arm, fewer rollouts than two independent arms would
need. Simulated cells are cheap enough to run in the hundreds.

The other axes follow the same logic. Perturbation axes are screened cheaply, which ranks them but
cannot establish that any one of them hurt, and certified at the cost of a single rate.
Unseen-object claims resample the object rather than the trial, so the count that matters is the
number of objects and not the number of rollouts. The transfer axis needs real and simulated
trials paired, and separating two correlations takes several hundred pairs, which is why it belongs
in a benchmark rather than in a paper. Every count there is a cap rather than a bill: a sequential
test on a graded score can decide in a fraction of the trials
\cite{beyond_binary_success_2026}, and a cell that stops early reports a confidence sequence rather
than a fixed-sample interval, never both.

\subsection{Feasibility and limitations}
\label{subsec:matrix}

The proposed protocol has not been run. In the twelve-method comparison assembled for this
survey, no method supplied all the measurements needed for a complete protocol row. DeXtreme
\cite{dextreme_2022}, for example, reports a success criterion and a confidence interval, but
uses ten trials. This limits retrospective comparison; it does not imply that the reported
experiments failed their own stated objectives.

\label{subsec:what_would_be_true}
The protocol is expensive. For each policy and task it calls for 57 matched anchor trials and 100
unseen-object trials. Comparing two policies on three tasks is then 342 real rollouts on the
matched set and 600 on the unseen-object set, 942 in all, or about 16 hours at one minute per
rollout before repairs and scoring. The same bill under an independent-arm design is 1200 rollouts
and 20 hours, so pairing saves about a fifth rather than the four fifths the pair counts suggest,
because a pair is still two rollouts and only the anchor set is paired. AutoEval
\cite{autoeval_2025} completed about 850 WidowX episodes in 24 hours with three interventions and
regular cooling pauses; a tendon-driven hand is likely to stretch the same workload across several
days.

That bill is large but not unprecedented. Evaluating one generalist policy against its baselines
has taken more than 2500 rollouts and more than 100 hours of human labour across three
institutions \cite{autoeval_2025}, and a recent careful examination analysed close to two thousand
real rollouts across nine hardware stations \cite{lbm_careful_examination_2025}. What is
unprecedented is paying that cost for a single dexterous-hand paper, where the modal cell is ten
trials.

Four things would have to change. Reviewers would have to reward 57 matched trials on one task
over 20 unmatched trials on five, and nothing in the corpus suggests that is happening. The loop
would have to be automated, and AutoEval \cite{autoeval_2025} shows it is buildable for a gripper
at one to three hours of setup, while stating that it supports binary success only and no
robustness axes. Somebody would have to run the penetration measure on their own rollouts, which
is a choice rather than a capability: Section~\ref{sec:sim:engines} shows the depth is computable
from the poses and the meshes in a few lines of Warp, and IsaacGymEnvs already ships a task that
does it every step. An earlier draft of this section made the engine the barrier, and that claim
is set aside, because the released code refutes it. The comparison would also have to be
sequential, because the savings in \cite{beyond_binary_success_2026} are the only reason 100 is a
cap rather than a cost.

The proposal has four limits. It has not been run, and its sample sizes come from power and
interval calculations rather than dexterous-hand experiments. The worst-case calculation
assumes a success rate of 0.5, while the paired design assumes a discordance rate of 0.3 that
the literature does not report. Its perturbations come from parallel-jaw benchmarks
\cite{colosseum_2024,simpler_2024}, where contact is simpler. Finally, the 2 mm penetration
threshold follows \cite{toporetarget_2026}; it is a simulator convention, not a physical limit.
Captured human grasps in GRAB \cite{grab_2020} average 3.25 mm under the same kind of
measurement. Establishing a meaningful threshold will require measurements across hands,
engines, and accepted behaviors.

Seven papers in this corpus are
evaluation protocols, a small denominator, and not one of them uses a dexterous hand: Badithela
et al. \cite{suresim_2025} run a parallel-jaw gripper and the other six state no hand.
Everything proposed above is therefore assembled from work on grippers and on whole-arm tasks.
A modest first test would run the protocol in Table~\ref{tab:protocol} once, on one in-hand
reorientation task with one 16-DoF hand, and release the rollouts and per-trial outcomes for
independent analysis.
